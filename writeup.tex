\begin{abstract}
We study the maximum independent set problem on sparse Erdős--Rényi graphs
$G(n,d/n)$ and on the SNAP ca-GrQc collaboration network.  Beyond reporting
the size of the independent set returned by an integer program, we pay close
attention to the gap between an incumbent solution and a certified optimum.
For $d=20$, random-order greedy tracks the $\log(d)n/d$ scale closely, whereas
minimum-degree greedy and IP warm starts recover noticeably larger independent
sets.  Across the random instances we tested, CBC certifies optimality only at
the smallest size, but the incumbent stays comfortably above the greedy scale
at every $n$.  On ca-GrQc, decomposing the graph by connected component brings
the exact IP within reach and yields $\alpha(G)=2459$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and Formulation}

For a graph $G=(V,E)$, an independent set is a subset $S\subseteq V$ containing
no adjacent pair.  The independence number is
\[
\alpha(G)=\max\{|S|: S\subseteq V,\; S\text{ independent}\}.
\]
The binary IP formulation introduces $x_v\in\{0,1\}$ for each vertex and solves
\[
\begin{aligned}
\max \quad & \sum_{v\in V}x_v\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & x_u+x_v\le 1, && (u,v)\in E,\\
& x_v\in\{0,1\}, && v\in V.
\end{aligned}
\]
These edge constraints are tight in the sense that any feasible binary vector
corresponds exactly to an independent set.

In practice we solve the equivalent minimum vertex-cover model.  Setting
$y_v=1-x_v$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\min \quad & \sum_{v\in V}y_v\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & y_u+y_v\ge 1, && (u,v)\in E,\\
& y_v\in\{0,1\}, && v\in V,
\end{aligned}
\]
and therefore
\[
\alpha(G)=|V|-\tau(G),
\]
where $\tau(G)$ is the minimum vertex-cover value.  We found CBC noticeably
more stable on the cover form once warm starts were enabled.  When CBC stops
before proving optimality, we report the bracket
\[
\alpha(G)\in [L,U],
\]
where $L$ is the incumbent independent set size and $U$ is the converted IP
upper bound.

\section{Random-Graph Benchmarks}

The project prompt motivates a classical comparison on sparse random graphs:
\[
\alpha(G(n,d/n))\sim \frac{2\log d}{d}n
\]
in the double limit $n\to\infty$ followed by $d\to\infty$, while a range of
simple greedy algorithms only reach sets of order $(\log d/d)n$ \cite{coja2015}.

\subsection{Greedy scale}

Random-order greedy gives a clean finite-$d$ heuristic.  Process the vertices
in a uniformly random order and accept each one whose neighborhood has not
already supplied an accepted vertex.  Let $s(t)$ be the accepted fraction after
a fraction $t$ of the vertices have been exposed.  In $G(n,d/n)$, a local
mean-field estimate gives
\[
\frac{\dd s}{\dd t}=\exp(-d s(t)),\qquad s(0)=0,
\]
since a fresh vertex is accepted exactly when none of its edges lands in the
already-accepted set, which has size roughly $s(t)n$.  Integrating yields
\[
s(t)=\frac{\log(1+dt)}{d},
\qquad
s(1)=\frac{\log(1+d)}{d}.
\]
For $d=20$ this evaluates to $0.1522$, essentially indistinguishable from
$\log(20)/20=0.1498$.  Wormald's differential-equation method is the standard
way to make this kind of mean-field approximation rigorous \cite{wormald1995}.

\subsection{First-moment upper benchmark}

The asymptotic value $2\log(d)/d$ is not very accurate numerically at $d=20$,
and a finite-$d$ first-moment bound gives a much sharper reference scale.  For
$k=an$,
\[
\E N_k
=
\binom{n}{k}(1-d/n)^{\binom{k}{2}},
\]
where $N_k$ is the number of independent sets of size $k$.  Stirling's formula
gives
\[
\frac{1}{n}\log \E N_{an}
=
H(a)-\frac{d a^2}{2}+o(1),
\]
where
\[
H(a)=-a\log a-(1-a)\log(1-a).
\]
Whenever $H(a)-d a^2/2<0$, Markov's inequality rules out independent sets of
size $an$ with high probability, so the largest positive root of
\[
H(a)-\frac{d a^2}{2}=0
\]
serves as a finite-$d$ upper benchmark.  At $d=20$ this root is
\[
a_{\mathrm{FM}}=0.232996,
\]
which makes clear that $2\log(20)/20=0.2996$ is best read as a large-$d$
asymptotic rather than a tight prediction at moderate $d$.

\section{Computational Design}

We compare three methods throughout.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Random-order greedy:} 20 random permutations in the notebook
run, and 100 in the long-batch runner.
\item \textbf{Minimum-degree greedy:} repeatedly accept a vertex of current
minimum degree and remove it together with its neighbors.
\item \textbf{IP/CBC:} solve the binary model in PuLP 3.3.0 with CBC 2.10.3,
warm-started from the best greedy solution available.
\end{itemize}
In the independent-set variables, every clique $C$ supplies the valid
inequality
\[
\sum_{v\in C}x_v\le 1,
\]
since at most one vertex of a clique can be picked.  Because we run the
vertex-cover model with $y_v=1-x_v$, triangle and 4-clique cuts enter in the
equivalent form
\[
\sum_{v\in C}y_v\ge |C|-1.
\]
We dropped these cuts on the real collaboration network: the graph already
carries many coauthorship cliques, and the bare edge formulation was effective
enough once the components had been split off.

Every returned set is checked directly against the edge list, and small
validation instances are additionally solved by exhaustive search; CBC matched
brute force on all three validation graphs,
\[
(n,d,\alpha)=(12,3,6),\quad (14,4,5),\quad (16,5,7).
\]

All experiments were run on an Apple M3 Pro with 18 GB RAM, Python 3.9.6,
PuLP 3.3.0, and CBC 2.10.3.  A resumable long-batch script accompanies the
notebook for the full grid; it writes one CSV row per instance and picks up
without overwriting completed runs.

\section{SNAP ca-GrQc}

Our real-network instance is the SNAP ca-GrQc collaboration graph
\cite{leskovec2007snap}, which records coauthorship in the arXiv General
Relativity and Quantum Cosmology category; a paper with $k$ authors contributes
a $k$-clique to the graph \cite{snapca}.  After symmetrizing duplicate directed
rows, dropping self-loops, and keeping loop-only vertices as isolated nodes,
the simple graph has
\[
n=5242,\qquad m=14484,\qquad \bar d=5.526.
\]
It splits into 355 connected components, the largest of which has 4158
vertices.  Since the independence number is additive over components, we
solve the IP one component at a time.

\section{Discussion}

The finite-$d$ picture differs visibly from the leading large-$d$ formula.
At $d=20$, the first-moment threshold sits near $0.233n$, well below
$2\log(d)n/d\approx 0.300n$.  Our IP incumbents on the short grid lie above
random-order greedy but still under this finite-$d$ upper benchmark, which is
the qualitative regime one expects.

The bottleneck is certification, not feasibility.  The minimum-degree warm
start almost always hands the IP a strong feasible solution immediately, but
CBC has a much harder time closing the upper-bound side of the certificate.
Put in branch-and-bound terms, the primal improves far more quickly than the
dual---this is the main algorithmic takeaway from the sparse random instances.

The real-network instance tells a different story.  Its 355 components
turn what would be a single 5242-node solve into many far smaller subproblems,
and the underlying coauthorship structure pushes minimum-degree greedy to
within one vertex of optimal.  In this regime the random-graph formulas serve
as baselines rather than predictions.

\section{Conclusion}

For $G(n,20/n)$, the warm-started IP pipeline reliably returns independent
sets beyond the random-order greedy scale.  Within the completed notebook
grid, exact certification only goes through at $n=100$; the larger instances
hit their time limits with nontrivial certificate gaps.  On the SNAP ca-GrQc
network, the same IP formulation, applied component by component, certifies
$\alpha(G)=2459$.

For broader experiments, the notebook ships with a resumable long-batch
runner that supports multi-seed extensions; the conclusions above are drawn
from the completed short-grid, matched-ER, and SNAP runs.
